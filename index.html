What the world calls each other

GermanyDeutschlandAllemagneNiemcySaksaVokietija德国

Pick a language and see what it calls every country on Earth. Pick a country and see what the world calls it. And, where the sources allow, what those names actually mean.

Dr Tristan Hotham, Independent researcher · October 2026 · Data: Wikidata (CC0); Wikipedia, CC-CEDICT and KANJIDIC2 (CC BY-SA 4.0); Natural Earth
54,037country names, one for each pair of 242 countries and territories and 262 languages that Wikidata covers
97%for the typical country, the share of the world's languages that call it something that sounds like its own name
17countries whose own name is echoed by fewer than a quarter of languages, among them Germany, Greece, Finland, Japan and China
83%of Chinese names for countries are chosen for their sound, not their meaning (163 of 196)

Germans call their country Deutschland. The French call it Allemagne, the Poles Niemcy, the Finns Saksa, the Lithuanians Vokietija and the Chinese Déguó, which, read character by character, comes out as "virtue country". Each of those names is a small piece of history: who met whom first, through which neighbours, and what they thought of each other. I wanted a map where you can see all of it at once.

Wikidata, the open database behind Wikipedia, stores the name of every country in hundreds of languages. I took the names of all 242 countries and territories on the Natural Earth world map in every language that names at least 100 of them: 262 languages and 32 writing systems. For the meanings I used Wikipedia's list of country-name etymologies and its articles on the names of Germany, China, Greece, Japan and others, and two open dictionaries for the Chinese and Japanese characters. Nothing here is my own etymology. Where no source gives a meaning, the page shows the name and its sound, nothing more.

      02 · Turning it roundWhat does the world call Germany?

      Germany is the champion of many names. I sorted its 261 Wikidata names into the six families described in Wikipedia's article on the names of Germany. Most languages, 137 of them, use a form of the Latin Germania, the Romans' word for the tribes beyond the Rhine. 58 follow the French Allemagne, from the Alamanni tribe: Spanish Alemania, Arabic Almānyā, Turkish Almanya. Only 37 use a form of the Germans' own word, Deutsch, from Old High German diutisc, "of the people": Dutch Duitsland, the Scandinavian Tyskland, Japanese ドイツ Doitsu, Korean 독일 Dogil, Vietnamese Đức and Chinese 德国 Déguó. 18 Slavic and neighbouring languages say Niemcy or Nemačka, from a Slavic word for the speechless or "the mute ones": people whose language the Slavs could not understand. Finnish (Saksa), Estonian and three smaller Finnic and Sami languages, 5 in all, call Germany after the Saxons, and Lithuanian and Latvian have a word of their own, Vokietija and Vācija, of unclear origin.

      The families are not my invention: each comes with a quoted sentence from its Wikipedia source, and the build checks every quote word for word. For the 109 languages that the article names under a family, my spelling rules put 99 in the article's family. The ten that differ are cases where the Wikidata label and the article point to different names: Bulgarian and Belarusian, for example, are listed in the article's Niemcy family, but their Wikidata labels are Германия and Германія, Germania forms.

      Some patterns jump out. Finland calls itself Suomi, but 243 of its 260 names are forms of the Old Norse "Finland"; only 14, echo Suomi, among them Estonian, Sami, Mari, Mordvin and Komi, its Uralic relatives, and the neighbouring Lithuanian and Latvian. Hungary is Magyarország at home, "land of the Magyars", and 38 languages follow it, from Czech and Croatian to Turkish (Macaristan), Arabic and Persian, while 194 use forms of Hungary, from a Turkic phrase for "ten arrows". The Netherlands is a tug of war: 142 languages borrow Nederland by sound, 42 translate it as "low countries" (Pays-Bas, Países Bajos) and 61 still call the whole country after one of its provinces, Holland. Egypt splits between the Greek Aigyptos, "home of the ka of Ptah" (192 languages), and the ancient Semitic Misr (67), the name Egyptians use themselves.

      04 · Virtue country, beautiful countryWhat the characters say, and what they don't

      Chinese writes foreign names with characters, and every character has a meaning. So it is tempting to read 美国, the United States, as "beautiful country", and 德国, Germany, as "virtue country". That reading is real, in the sense that a Chinese reader sees those meanings, but it is not where the names come from. 美 is short for 美利堅 Měilìjiān, an early transcription of "America"; 德 is short for 德意志 Déyìzhì, a transcription of "Deutsch". The characters were chosen for their sound, and often for being pleasant to read, which is why so many foreign names in Chinese sound flattering.

      I tagged every character in every Chinese country name as either sound or meaning. Of 196 sovereign countries, 163 have names made purely of sound characters, 20 mix sound with a meaning element such as 南 south, 新 new or 岛 island, 5 are short forms like 德国 and 美国, and only 2 are true translations: 冰岛 Iceland, "ice island", and 黑山 Montenegro, "black mountain". 6 are the countries' own names written in characters, such as 日本 and 越南.       Japanese mostly sidesteps the question. 168 of 196 countries (86%) are written in katakana, a script that records sound only: ドイツ Doitsu for Germany, イギリス Igirisu for Britain, from the Portuguese inglês. But older kanji forms survive as short forms, and those do carry meanings: 米国 Beikoku for the United States, which Wikipedia notes "literally translates to 'rice country'" (米 is taken from 亜米利加 A-me-ri-ka), 独 for Germany, 露国 for Russia, 豪州 for Australia, 比島 for the Philippines and 氷島 for Iceland, "ice island" again. Korean and Vietnamese often read the same characters in their own way: Korean 미국 Miguk and 독일 Dogil are 美國 and 獨逸; Vietnamese Đức (Germany) is 德, and Hà Lan (the Netherlands) is 荷蘭. I matched 10 Korean and 17 Vietnamese names to their characters this way, using KANJIDIC2's readings.

      05 · Your own nameWhat does your country's name mean?

      Turn from what others call a country to what it calls itself. Wikipedia's list of country-name etymologies gives an origin for 193 of the 196 sovereign states on the map. I sorted them, by reading each entry, into rough kinds. The largest group, 60, are descriptions: "united states", "rich coast" (Costa Rica), "black mountain" (Montenegro), "equator" (Ecuador), "edge, borderland" (Ukraine). 47 are named after a people: Poland after the Polans, France after the Franks, Finland after the Finns. 42 take the name of a river, city or other place: India from the Indus, Zambia from the Zambezi, Zimbabwe from the stone houses of Great Zimbabwe. 11 are named after a person, such as Colombia (Columbus), Bolivia (Simón Bolívar), the Philippines (Philip II of Spain) and Saudi Arabia (the House of Saud), and 9 after a saint or Christ, such as El Salvador, "the Saviour". For 16, among them Peru, Cuba and Syria, the list says the origin is uncertain.

      06 · StoriesOld names that refuse to die

      China, Cathay and Zhongguo

      China · Kitai · 中国
      Most of the world, 207 languages, says some form of "China", often traced to the Qin state, which went on to unify China. But 32 languages, Russian (Kitai) and many in Central Asia among them, still use the name of the Khitan people, the "Cathay" of Marco Polo. 17 echo China's own Zhongguo, "Middle Kingdom".
      List of country-name etymologies; Names of China.

      Greece, Hellas and Yunan

      Greece · Yunanistan · Ελλάδα
      The Greeks call their country Hellas (Ελλάδα), but only 18 languages follow them. 190 use the Latin Graecia. 42, from Arabic and Turkish to Hindi, say Yunan, from the Old Persian Yauna for the Ionian Greeks.
      Names of Greece; List of country-name etymologies.

      Wales and the foreigners

      Wales · Wallonia · Wallachia · Włochy
      "Wales" comes from the Old English Wēalas, from a Germanic word, *Walhaz, first used for a Gaulish people and then for anyone from the Roman world. The same root names Wallonia, Wallachia and Włochy, the Polish name for Italy. In my data, 2 languages call Italy by a form of it: Polish Włochy and Hungarian Olaszország.
      Names of Wales; List of country-name etymologies.

      Persia becomes Iran

      Persia → Iran · ایران
      Iranians have long called their country Iran. In 1935 Reza Shah asked foreign delegates to use "Iran", not Persia, in formal correspondence. The change stuck: of 256 Wikidata names for Iran, only 2 still look like Persia: Amharic (ፋርስ, Fars) and Old English.
      Names of Iran.

      Holland or the Netherlands?

      Nederland · Holland · Pays-Bas
      Holland is one region of the Netherlands, yet 61 of 258 languages use it for the whole country, from Japanese オランダ Oranda to Chinese 荷兰 Hélán. Indonesian and Malay say Belanda.
      Names of the Netherlands; List of country-name etymologies.

      Nippon, Cipangu, Japan

      日本 · Nihon · Japan
      日本 means "sun origin", the land where the sun rises, seen from China. Marco Polo recorded a Chinese pronunciation of it as Cipangu around 1300; later, Portuguese traders in Malacca heard the Malay Jepang and carried the word west, where it became "Japan". 238 languages use a form of Japan; 21, mostly in East and South East Asia, read the characters 日本 their own way.
      Names of Japan; List of country-name etymologies.

      Named after people

      Colombia · Bolivia · the Philippines
      Colombia is the "land of Columbus" and Bolivia the "land of Bolívar". The Philippines honour Philip II of Spain and Saudi Arabia the House of Saud. America itself is named after the explorer Amerigo Vespucci, on a map of 1507 by Martin Waldseemüller.
      List of country-name etymologies; Names of the United States.

      Named after the land

      Montenegro · Iceland · Argentina
      Montenegro is "black mountain" in Venetian, for Mount Lovćen and its dark forests; Iceland is "land of ice"; Ecuador is the equator; Argentina comes from the Italian for "of silver"; Costa Rica is the "rich coast"; Cameroon is named after the shrimp of the Wouri river.

      07 · Called by another nameWhich countries does the world rename?

      For most countries the world simply borrows the local name and bends it to its own sounds: Kenya, Peru, Canada. For the typical country, 97% of the other languages use a name that starts with the same consonant sounds as its own, and 120 of the 196 sovereign states are echoed by 95% or more. But 21 are called something different by more than half the world, and 17 by more than three quarters. Among them: Armenia (Hayastan), Albania (Shqipëria), Bhutan (Druk Yul), Greece (Hellas), Georgia (Sakartvelo), Finland (Suomi), Croatia (Hrvatska), Montenegro (Crna Gora), Japan (Nihon), India (Bharat), Germany (Deutschland), Hungary (Magyarország) and Egypt (Misr).

      The countries the world calls something else
      Share of the other core languages (those naming 200 or more places) whose name for the country sounds like the country's own name. The 22 lowest of 196 sovereign states.
      A few results reflect spelling more than history: Jordan (al-Urdun), the Czech Republic (Česko, Tschechien) and the Central African Republic (Centrafrique, Zentralafrika) sit low partly because the automatic test misses their shared roots.

      Turn the question round and ask which languages rename the world most. Among the 16 languages with 100 million speakers or more, Chinese stands out: in its traditional and simplified forms, only 66% and 68% of its country names start with the same sounds as the country's own name, against a median of 86% for all core languages. Part of the reason is that Chinese writes foreign sounds with its own syllables and shortens long names: Germany becomes Dé, America Měi, England Yīng. Indonesian is at the other end of the big languages (90%).

      08 · MethodsHow I did this, and what to watch for

      In short

      • Countries. The 242 countries and territories of Natural Earth's 1:50m map, of which 196 are sovereign states (Wikidata "sovereign state", plus Denmark). Each is matched to its Wikidata item by the identifier Natural Earth provides.
      • Names. The Wikidata label of each country in each language. I kept the 262 language codes with names for at least 100 of the 242 places; 183 "core" languages name 200 or more. Wikidata labels are written by volunteers; they are usually the standard modern name, but occasionally an older, rarer or full official form. The language list includes historic and minority languages (Old English, Latin, Cornish) alongside national ones, because they are in Wikidata; speaker numbers come from Wikidata too and are missing for 5.
      • Own name. The label in the country's main official language (Wikidata, with a few fixes listed in the code), plus its native label. "Sounds like its own name" compares the first consonant sounds of the romanised names, after dropping words like "republic" and "kingdom". It is crude: it is right for 95.8% of the 3,622 names that I also sorted by hand into families (53 false matches, 98 misses).
      • Families. For fourteen countries, families of names and their origins are taken from Wikipedia, each with a quoted sentence that the build checks word for word; names are assigned to families by spelling rules on their romanised forms, and the rest go to "other". On the reverse map, countries without hand-built families are grouped automatically by the first two consonant sounds of the name, which gathers spellings but says nothing about origins.
      • Meanings. English-name and own-name etymologies come from Wikipedia's "List of country-name etymologies"; the kinds (description, people, place, person, saint, state, uncertain) are my sorting after reading every entry. Chinese characters are glossed with CC-CEDICT and tagged as sound or meaning using a short list of meaning elements and hand-checked whole names; Japanese kanji are glossed with KANJIDIC2. A gloss of a sound character is what the character means on its own, not the meaning of the name.

      Caveats

      • One label per language hides variety: many languages have several names for a country, formal and informal, old and new.
      • "Language" here means a Wikimedia language edition, not a population: Old English counts once, like Spanish. Read the counts as counts of languages, not of people.
      • The main-language map assigns one language per country, so multilingual countries (India, Switzerland, much of Africa) show only one of their languages.
      • Romanisations are automatic (anyascii) and rough, especially for Arabic and other scripts that do not write vowels.
